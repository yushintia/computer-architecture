# Week 14 Presentation Rubric

Computer Architecture (503872-004). This is the full grading rubric for
the Week 14 team presentation, 10% of the final grade (see Week 1's
grading table). The slide deck shows a summary; this document is the
complete version, for students to check before and after presenting.

---

## 1. What You Are Presenting

Each team picks **one real, current chip or system** (a phone chip, a
laptop chip, a GPU, a game console, or a cloud server) and explains:

1. Which parallel technique(s) it uses: pipelining, superscalar
   execution, multicore, SIMD, multithreading, or GPU-style parallel
   execution
2. Evidence that the technique is really there: a spec sheet, a die
   photo, a datasheet, or a published benchmark
3. What a user actually gains from it: speed, battery life, or cost

This closes the exact gap Week 13 left open: parallel techniques named
on paper, not yet seen working in one real design.

---

## 2. Point Breakdown (100 points total)

| Criterion | Points | What earns full marks |
|---|---|---|
| Clarity | 25 | A classmate with no notes can follow the explanation start to finish, in plain language, with jargon defined before it is used |
| Technical accuracy | 35 | The parallel technique is named correctly, described correctly, and matches the evidence shown |
| Vocabulary use | 15 | This semester's terms (ISA, microarchitecture, pipeline, cache, core, throughput, etc.) are used, and used correctly, not just dropped in |
| Q&A handling | 25 | Every question gets a direct answer; if the team does not know, they say so instead of guessing confidently |

---

## 3. Criterion Detail

### Clarity (25 points)

| Band | Points | Description |
|---|---|---|
| Excellent | 22-25 | The pain-to-payoff arc is obvious. A first-time listener understands the chip's parallelism without re-asking |
| Good | 16-21 | Mostly clear, one or two moments need a follow-up question to understand |
| Needs work | 8-15 | The audience follows the slides but not the reasoning behind them |
| Weak | 0-7 | The audience cannot restate what was presented, even roughly |

### Technical accuracy (35 points)

| Band | Points | Description |
|---|---|---|
| Excellent | 30-35 | The named technique is correct, the evidence supports it, and the team can defend the claim under a follow-up question |
| Good | 21-29 | The technique is mostly correct, with a small factual slip that does not change the conclusion |
| Needs work | 11-20 | The technique is misnamed or the evidence does not clearly support the claim |
| Weak | 0-10 | The claimed technique is wrong, or no real evidence is shown |

### Vocabulary use (15 points)

| Band | Points | Description |
|---|---|---|
| Excellent | 13-15 | Terms from this semester are used precisely and where they actually apply |
| Good | 9-12 | Terms are used correctly most of the time, with one or two loose uses |
| Needs work | 4-8 | Terms are used but noticeably imprecise, e.g. "core" and "thread" swapped |
| Weak | 0-3 | Little or no use of this semester's vocabulary |

### Q&A handling (25 points)

| Band | Points | Description |
|---|---|---|
| Excellent | 22-25 | Every question gets a direct, on-topic answer; uncertainty is stated honestly rather than guessed past |
| Good | 16-21 | Most questions are answered well, one answer drifts off-topic |
| Needs work | 8-15 | Answers are vague, or the team repeats the presentation instead of answering |
| Weak | 0-7 | Questions go unanswered, or answers are confidently wrong |

---

## 4. Format Reminders

- 12 minutes per team: 8 minutes presenting, 4 minutes questions
- Every team member must speak; a silent member loses clarity points
- A visible timer enforces the 8-minute mark; questions start whether
  or not the team has finished
- Bring slides on a USB drive, on a laptop as backup, and as a cloud
  link, in case either fails live

---

## 5. Common Ways Teams Lose Points

- **Listing features instead of explaining them.** "It has 8 cores" is
  a fact, not an explanation. Say what those 8 cores let the chip do
  at once, and why that helps
- **No real evidence.** A claim about a chip's design needs a spec
  sheet, datasheet, or benchmark behind it, not just a guess
- **Guessing instead of admitting uncertainty in Q&A.** A confident
  wrong answer scores lower than an honest "we are not sure, but here
  is our best reasoning"
- **Over-scoping.** A small, well-explained example beats a large,
  half-explained one every time

## 6. After Your Presentation

Keep your own team's Q&A questions. Week 15 is a short review week
covering Weeks 1 through 14; the questions your presentation could not
fully answer are a good place to start reviewing.
